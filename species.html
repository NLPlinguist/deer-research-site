All the Deer of North America

Not every deer looks the same. Deer belong to the family Cervidae, and across North America you can find several different species. Some live in green forests, some in dry deserts, and some in snowy tundra. Let's meet every one of them.

What Kind of Animal Is a Deer?

Here are the important facts your report needs about what kind of animal a deer is:

  • Vertebrate: Yes! Deer are vertebrates, which means they have a backbone (a spinal column made of small bones).
  • Mammal: Deer belong to the class Mammalia. Mammals have fur or hair, give birth to live babies, and feed their babies milk. Deer have all three of these traits.
  • Warm-blooded: Deer are warm-blooded (scientists call this “endotherms”). Their body stays warm inside, even when it is cold outside. They make their own heat.
  • Four legs: Deer walk on four legs and have five toes on each foot.
  • Herbivore: Deer eat plants. They are plant-eaters, not meat-eaters.

So for your RIGHT panel, you can say: “A deer is a vertebrate mammal that is warm-blooded and eats plants.” That covers everything the assignment asks.

White-Tailed Deer (Odocoileus virginianus)

The white-tailed deer is the most common deer in North America, found from southern Canada down through the United States and into Central America. It has a brown coat in summer that turns grayish in winter. When it is scared, it flips up its tail to show a bright white flash — that is how it gets its name. Baby white-tailed deer (called fawns) are born with white spots on their backs that help them hide in the leafy forest.

Did you know? Male white-tailed deer grow new antlers every year. The old ones fall off in late winter, and new ones grow in spring and summer. Antlers are made of a special bone-like material.

White-tailed deer are fast and good swimmers. They can run up about 30 miles per hour. See more speed facts →

Mule Deer (Odocoileus hemionus)

Mule deer live mainly in the western United States, from the Great Plains to the Pacific Coast, up into western Canada. They are named for their big, donkey-like (mule-like) ears. Their antlers point backward and then fork, which is very different from the white-tailed deer’s antlers. Mule deer can live in forests, mountains, dry fields, and even deserts.

Mule deer are famous for a special running style called “stotting” — they leap up with all four feet off the ground at the same time. That is one of the ways predators know a deer is nearby.

Black-Tailed Deer (Odocoileus hemionus columbianus)

The black-tailed deer is actually a type of mule deer that lives in the Pacific Northwest — Washington, Oregon, and northern California. It has a darker coat and a white ring around the base of its tail, which gives it its name. It lives in dense forests near the coast, and it is more shy than mule deer.

Key Deer (Odocoileus virginianus clavium)

The key deer is the smallest deer in North America and it is endangered. It lives only in the Florida Keys — a chain of islands south of Florida. The key deer looks like a tiny white-tailed deer, and there are only a few hundred of them left in the wild. Scientists track their population carefully because their home is threatened by storms, development, and rising seas.

Elk (Cervus canadensis)

Elk are the largest deer in North America. A bull elk can weigh more than 500 pounds. Elk live in mountains, forests, and open grasslands, especially in western North America — think of Yellowstone National Park. In the fall, male elk make a very loud roaring sound called a bugle to attract females and challenge other males. Elk also migrate: in summer they graze on high mountain meadows, and in winter they move downhill to find food and shelter from the snow.

Moose (Alces alces)

Moose are the largest deer in the world. They are dark brown, very strong, and love to swim and eat plants around lakes and streams. Moose antlers are wide and flat, almost like a crown. Female moose usually have one calf at a time. Moose live in cold northern forests (the boreal forest) and can run surprisingly fast — up to about 35 miles per hour for short distances. Their big hooves work like snowshoes in winter.

Caribou (Rangifer tarandus)

Caribou are the northern deer. In North America they are found in Alaska and across northern Canada. What makes caribou special is that both males and females grow antlers — this is unusual among deer. Caribou live on the tundra (a frozen, treeless place) and in the boreal forest. They travel very long distances — some herds migrate hundreds or even more than a thousand miles to follow the seasons and find food. Their wide, concave hooves help them walk on soft snow and dig through it to find lichen (a type of plant).
